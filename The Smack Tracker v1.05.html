how to use this simulator

Dose is the amount you take each time. Interval is how many hours between doses. Together they determine how much buprenorphine accumulates in your system and where it stabilizes — the steady state the chart converges to after roughly 5 half-lives (~8 days at default settings).

Ceiling is the upper limit you're trying to stay below — set it to your current stable peak if you're tapering, or to a clinical threshold if you have one. Prior steady state is where you're coming from. The chart shows what happens to your blood level when you switch from the old schedule to the new one.

To plan a reduction: lower the dose or lengthen the interval, watch the SS Peak metric, and check that the new peak falls where you want it. Small reductions in dose have a roughly linear effect on SS level. Lengthening the interval has a larger effect at short intervals and flattens out as intervals approach the half-life.

Note on peaks and troughs: buprenorphine's half-life is long (~37hr), so the level stays high between doses. At 24hr dosing with a 37hr half-life the trough sits around 64% of the peak (it equals peak × e−ke·τ); as you shorten the interval, peak and trough converge. The steady-state peak follows the standard accumulation ratio, 1 ÷ (1 − e−ke·τ) × dose — so 750 mcg once daily settles at about 2,070 mcg. This is why splitting a dose feels smoother than one large dose even at the same daily total.

This graph is a pharmacokinetic estimate based on a one-compartment oral model with population-average parameters. Individual response varies. What actually matters is whether your dose silences cravings and keeps withdrawal at bay — that's between you and your clinician, and no model replaces that feedback loop.

film cutting guide — actual size
Print from a desktop or laptop browser. Mobile browsers do not support the precision printing this guide requires. Open this file in Chrome or Firefox on a computer, then use the print button below. Do not scale, shrink-to-fit, or fit-to-page — print at exactly 100%.
Note: The 2mg and 8mg films are physically identical in size (22×12.8mm). They differ only in drug concentration — the 8mg film contains 3.2× more buprenorphine per square millimeter. A 1/8 cut of a 2mg film = 250mcg. A 1/8 cut of an 8mg film = 1000mcg. Always verify the imprint before cutting.
how to cut accurately

Select the film strength below, hit print, and print at exactly 100% scale — no fit-to-page, no shrink-to-fit. The printed guide will be the exact physical size of the film. Verify with a ruler: the strip should measure exactly 22mm wide before you touch it with a blade.

Use a sharp hobby knife with a fresh blade. A dull blade drags and tears the film instead of cutting it. One new blade costs less than a wasted strip. X-Acto #11 or a snap-off utility blade both work. Replace the blade if you feel any resistance.

Cut on glass. A smooth glass surface — a picture frame insert, a glass cutting board — gives you a flat, hard backing with no give. Cutting on cardboard or a soft mat lets the blade wander. The film won't slip on glass, which matters at this scale.

Put your eyes where the blade needs to go, not where it is. Look ahead of the cut along your guide line. Your hand follows your eyes. Looking at the blade itself is what produces drift.

Several light quick strokes, not one heavy push. Score the film with two or three passes of minimal pressure before committing to a full cut. Light passes stay on line. A single heavy stroke wants to skate off it. Once you feel the blade reach the glass, one clean final pass finishes it.

Place the printed guide under the film if the film is translucent enough, or lay it alongside as a measuring reference and use a metal straight edge — never a plastic ruler — aligned to the grid lines. The drug is uniformly distributed throughout the film matrix, so a geometrically accurate cut is a dosimetrically accurate cut.

on film vs tablet
Films are the better choice for precision tapering. The drug is distributed uniformly throughout the film matrix, so a geometrically accurate cut produces a proportionally accurate dose. Tablets cannot be cut accurately — their active ingredient is not uniformly distributed, and the cut surface area introduces variability. This app is designed around films.

CLINICIAN ACCESS

This feature allows a clinician to track patient dose history using an Instance ID provided voluntarily by the patient.

The app is designed to empower users, not to enable clinician surveillance. Users have full control over whether they share their information. Ideally, this is done within a mutually agreed-upon therapeutic relationship built on trust and professional ethics consistent with the Hippocratic tradition.

The patient is responsible for their own tracking and logging. If a patient loses their local data, the clinician may share what they have on file as a last resort — but this is not a substitute for the patient maintaining their own backup.
